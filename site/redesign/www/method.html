How the Screening Works

A transparent, rule-based model compares candidate public EV charging sites, sizes a starter installation for each, and flags what needs review before any decision moves forward. It supports planning; it approves nothing.

The Nine Steps

The model is built for public-sector planners and local government staff. It helps them compare candidate sites side by side, pick starter candidates, see where future expansion may be needed, and spot administrative or engineering review issues early.

It runs as a fixed sequence, so every result can be traced back through these steps. Open a step to read what happens there.

  1. Candidate public EV charging locations are loaded into the model. This run covers twelve sites in Fairfax and Prince William County.

  2. Each site is described with planning-screen inputs such as site type, public access, operating hours, parking context, nearby charging supply and electrical assumptions.

    What Goes In

  3. The model scores four weighted parts: demand (45 points), feasibility (30), safety (15) and equity (10). Each part is built from named drivers with published weights.

    How Sites Are Scored

  4. Each candidate receives a planning-support readiness score. The score exists for comparison between sites, not as a measure of approval.

  5. Scores and review signals are translated into planning categories: advance, expandable starter, resolve review issues, monitor, or do not advance yet.

    Decision Categories

  6. The model separates install-now ports from future-ready capacity, so a site can start small without closing off growth.

    Right-Sizing Ports

  7. The model identifies fields or site conditions that need confirmation before implementation decisions.

    Baseline Check

  8. Alternative future-demand conditions are tested to see whether future-ready capacity remains adequate.

    Scenario Testing

  9. The model exports site rankings, decision buckets, Tableau-ready files, scenario results and the generated PDF planning report.

    Outputs and Limits

What Goes In

Each site is described by planning-screen inputs in six groups. They are early-stage planning attributes: some still need formal confirmation, and none is treated as final, verified engineering data.

Inputs stay private. Only outputs are published.

The real values behind these inputs, such as electrical service ratings, spare capacity, visitor and employee counts and parking counts, appear nowhere on this site. Pages show only what the model produces: ranks, scores, categories, port plans and review needs.

Input Categories
  • Site identitySite ID, site name and jurisdiction.
  • Context and usePublic-serving use type, operating hours and parking context.
  • Demand proxiesHouseholds and jobs nearby, traffic on the nearest arterial, nearby amenities, local EV share and the charging already nearby. Visitor and employee counts size the number of ports; they do not change the readiness score.
  • InfrastructureLikely charger placement, electrical service rating and spare electrical capacity.
  • Safety and complianceADA and accessibility considerations, emergency disconnect visibility, garage and structure-specific needs, fire and AHJ indicators, and stormwater and site-work indicators.
  • Testing conditionsFuture demand scenarios.
Site inputs as of 2026-06-24.

How Sites Are Scored

Each site's readiness score, out of 100, adds up four weighted parts. Each part is scored from 0 to 100 from named drivers, then counts for its share of the total. Every weight is published here, exactly as the model uses it.

  • 45 points

    Demand

    Whether the site is likely to attract enough public charging: the people, jobs, traffic and destinations around it, how long and how often people visit, and how much charging is already nearby.

  • 30 points

    Feasibility

    How hard and costly the electrical work looks: spare capacity, how far chargers would sit from the electrical service, the estimated installation cost and the length of trenching.

  • 15 points

    Safety

    Whether an accessible (ADA) layout and fire access are possible, whether chargers would sit in an enclosed garage, whether the emergency disconnect would be visible, and stormwater risk.

  • 10 points

    Equity

    How underserved the surrounding area is for charging, from a 0 to 1 equity gap index.

Rules applied with the weights

  • Access adjustment. A site without general public access has its demand part counted at 65 percent. In this run that applies to one site, the Public Safety Training Center (PWC003).
  • Fixed reference points. Each driver is scaled from 0 to 1 against fixed reference values before it is weighted, so a site's score does not change when other sites are added or removed. Hours open are measured against a 24-hour day.
  • A missing value earns nothing. A value nobody recorded gives no credit in either direction. It is never read as a good answer.
  • Future readiness is not part of the score. It is handled after scoring, by right-sizing ports and scenario testing. Accessibility is not a separate part either: whether an accessible layout is possible is one of the Safety drivers.

Each site's score is broken into these four parts, in points, on its Sites page and on the Compare page.

How Stable the Ranks Are

The weights are planning judgements, not measured facts. To see how much the order depends on them, the sites were rescored with the weights moved. The two leading sites are close: FFX004 scores 53.4 and FFX002 scores 51.8. The 1.6-point gap between FFX004 and FFX002 is within what modest changes to the weights can reverse.

One weight at a time

Each part's weight was moved 5 points up, then 5 points down. The other three weights are scaled in proportion, so the four still add up to 100. Only one of the eight changes puts a different site first: 5 more points of Equity puts FFX002 first. 5 fewer points of Demand leaves FFX004 first by a tenth of a point (53.1 against 53.0). The smallest single change that puts a different site first is Equity rising from 10 to about 13.0 points.

The scores for each change appear here when the page's data loads.

Many weightings at random

The four weights were also drawn at random 2,000 times around their published values (Demand between about 34 and 57, Feasibility between about 20 and 41, Safety between about 8 and 24, Equity between about 4 and 18, in 9 of 10 draws), and the sites were rescored every time. A site's chance of first place is the share of those weightings in which it came first. It is a share of plausible weightings, not the chance that a site is the right place to build.

The chance of first place for each site appears here when the page's data loads.

What this check does not cover

  • Uncertain inputs. Data confidence is Low at all 12 sites. The chances above move the weights only and treat every input as correct, so errors in the inputs could move the ranks more than any weight change tested here.
  • Placeholder electrical capacity. Spare capacity is an unverified placeholder at all 12 sites and is worth up to 10.5 points, so 7 sites share exactly the same feasibility points (12.6). If capacity alone ran anywhere from no credit to full credit, 9 of the 12 sites could rank first; with every unconfirmed input allowed to move, any of the 12 could. That is a range of what could happen, not a probability. Confirming capacity is the step that would firm up the order most.
  • The other rules. The driver weights inside each part, the tier cut-offs and the access adjustment stay as published.

Decision Categories

The scores and review signals sort every site into one of five categories. The categories help a team triage quickly: which sites warrant detailed engineering study now, and which should wait.

  • Tier 1

    Advance as strong starter candidates

    The strongest current starter candidates in the model, with solid demand and lower implementation friction.

  • Tier 2

    Good starter candidates with expandable design

    These sites may support a starter installation well, but should be planned carefully with future expansion and verification in mind.

  • High review

    Resolve review issues before expansion

    These sites may be useful public assets, but significant review burdens or electrical constraints should be addressed before committing to major capacity.

  • Tier 3

    Monitor or run a small pilot

    These sites should be monitored over time, or considered only for smaller pilot-style planning rather than immediate general expansion.

  • Special use

    Do not advance for general-public buildout yet

    The site should not advance as a general-public charging site unless its foundational assumptions change, for example its public access hours or a fleet-only use case.

Right-Sizing Ports

The aim is to avoid overbuilding while still preparing each site for growth. The model does this by splitting capacity into two recommendations.

25

Install-now ports

A smaller starter recommendation based on immediate feasibility, electrical capacity and modeled current demand.

47

Future-ready ports

The capacity a site should be prepared to accommodate if demand grows, such as conduit, panel space and spatial planning.

Four things to know about the published plan

  • It follows the official milestone2_v3 ranking. Its totals of 25 install-now and 47 future-ready ports are the official plan, the same ranking the results pages show.
  • Why the total is 47, not the earlier 48. The earlier result (legacy_v2) sized ports on the earlier ranking and had 48 future-ready ports. On the current ranking, Pohick Regional Library (FFX003) sits a tier lower, so it is prepared for 2 future-ready ports instead of 4, and PWC McCoart Government Center (PWC001) sits a tier higher, so it is prepared for 4 instead of 3. The change comes from the ranking, not from the rule that sizes future-ready ports: either version of that rule gives 47 on today's ranking and 48 on the earlier one.
  • Every site gets at least 2 install-now ports. That includes the Public Safety Training Center (PWC003), where the advice is not to advance a public buildout until the use case is confirmed. Its 2 ports are counted in the total as a placeholder, not as a recommendation.
  • Chinn Park Regional Library (PWC004) was closed for renovation when the site data were gathered in June 2026. Its rank and plan assume it reopens much as it was.

Right-sizing is a planning-support recommendation. It must always be checked against engineering, utility, procurement, site design, ADA and permitting review. Electrical capacity is an unverified placeholder at all twelve sites.

Scenario Testing

To check that future-ready recommendations hold up, the model tests whether the future-ready capacity stays adequate as demand grows in different ways. The canonical milestone2_v3 model defines three growth scenarios from the 2026 baseline.

Scenarios
  • BaseDemand grows 1.9 times by 2036, 3.0 times by 2046 and 4.0 times by 2056.
  • High growthDemand grows 2.6 times by 2036, 4.5 times by 2046 and 6.0 times by 2056.
  • Combined stressHigh growth plus a further 15 percent at every horizon.
These growth figures are provisional planning conventions, not yet calibrated to observed charging data. See Before Funding Decisions.

Official result (milestone2_v3)

Of the 47 future-ready ports, base growth has 31 active by 2036, 43 by 2046 and 43 by 2056. High growth and combined stress both have 43 active by 2036, all 47 by 2046 and all 47 by 2056.

In this result each site's active ports are capped at its future-ready ports, so it does not count capacity gaps the way the earlier sweep did. See the ports over time.

Earlier result (legacy_v2)

The earlier legacy_v2 sweep tested six scenarios: base, high growth and combined stress (the growth figures above are the canonical definitions of those names), plus long sessions, a short daily window and peakier demand, whose parameters are not published here. Five showed no future-ready capacity gaps at any site. Combined stress opened two minor gaps of one port each.

Review flags are not failures

The earlier run had zero blocking validation errors. Each of its 38 review flags marks a field that needs more information or administrative confirmation from local staff before detailed design can proceed. The fields that recur most are:

  • Electrical service ratings
  • Spare electrical capacity

  • Likely charger placement
  • Garage, fire, AHJ and access reviews at special-use sites

Before Using This for Funding Decisions

The 2036 to 2056 scenarios and the lifecycle cost comparisons rest on demand growth assumptions that have not been checked against real charging data. They are fine for comparing options. Before any of them supports a funding decision, test them against evidence like this.

  1. 1

    Operating charger utilization

    Compare the modelled sessions and waits with how busy real public chargers are. The closest local evidence is the client's own operating data: Prince William County already runs a large charging installation at the Neabsco-Potomac Commuter Garage (PWC002), where some stations were later taken offline. Its session records show how often ports are used and how many sit idle. Exports from any other county-run chargers help too.

  2. 2

    Observed dwell times

    Port counts and waits depend on how long cars stay plugged in. Replace the assumed visit lengths with measured session lengths from the same charger records, site by site where possible.

  3. 3

    Installation-cost evidence

    The cost comparisons are screening-class estimates built from national cost benchmarks (AFDC and NREL cost classes) and the client's experience. They are not quotes. Check them against the counties' own recent installation costs, such as the trenching costs on the McCoart Government Center installation, and against contractor quotes for the shortlisted sites.

  4. 4

    Utility capacity confirmation

    Electrical capacity is an unverified placeholder at all 12 sites. Ask the electric utility serving each site, and each facility manager, to confirm the service rating and spare capacity before sizing or funding anything.

  5. 5

    Public charging already nearby

    Where it applies, use the Alternative Fuels Data Center (AFDC) station data, maintained by NREL for the U.S. Department of Energy, to confirm what public charging exists near each site. It lists stations and ports, not how busy they are, so it cannot stand in for utilization data.

The model is built to take this evidence in: it has templates for recording site-day charger sessions and capacity confirmations, and a rerun carries the new evidence into the results with its source recorded. Recalibrate the growth assumptions once a year or more of local operating data is in hand, then rerun the scenarios and the cost comparison.

Outputs and Limits

What It Produces

  • Public EV Charging Planning Report (PDF)
  • Site rankings
  • Decision buckets
  • Port recommendations
  • Scenario results
  • Visual summaries
  • Tableau-ready CSV exports
Built in Python with pandas, NumPy and simple rule-based scoring logic. Intended for early screening, prioritization, right-sizing discussion and identifying review needs.

What It Does Not Replace

  • Final engineering design
  • Utility coordination
  • Formal permitting
  • Procurement processes
  • ADA compliance review
  • Fire and AHJ review
  • Stormwater review
  • Construction approval
  • On-the-ground site surveys
  • Stakeholder confirmation
  • Official municipal decision-making
Outputs support early screening, prioritization and discussion. They are not a final approval, a final design or a construction-ready determination.

A planning-support prototype

This Future City Fellowship prototype is not engineering advice, a permit or code-compliance determination, a utility capacity confirmation, an official forecast, or a procurement or construction authorization. Every siting decision remains subject to electrical, ADA, fire and AHJ, stormwater and permitting review by the responsible authorities. No site shown here is approved.

Data confidence is Low at all 12 sites. Electrical capacity is an unverified placeholder at every one of them: nobody has confirmed the service could support chargers.
